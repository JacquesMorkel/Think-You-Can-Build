# Think You Can Build

## Project documentation

**Working tagline:** Show how you solve business problems  
**Challenge:** Graduate Employability  
**Project stage:** Concept and hackathon prototype planning  
**Founder/team:** Individual founder at this stage  
**Geography:** South Africa (proposed initial context)

> **Status note:** No product, user research, employer partnerships, or pilot results have been completed yet. This document describes the concept, current evidence, assumptions, proposed MVP, and a plan to test it. It does not claim that the service has already improved hiring outcomes.

## 1. Executive summary

Think You Can Build is a proposed live problem-solving show and graduate talent showcase. In each challenge, three to five graduates make individual attempts at the same computer-based business case while working in one facilitated call. The facilitator acts like a senior colleague or project lead: they introduce the case, ask questions, prompt participants to explain their reasoning, and make their work visible as they test possible solutions.

The first cases will be fictional or carefully adapted mock-company scenarios based on common business problems. The project may later invite companies to submit challenges and negotiate a bounty before a challenge is published. A launch goal is to approach at least 20 companies during the first month; this is a target, not a secured partner list.

The intended value is two-sided. Graduates can demonstrate problem understanding, reasoning, feasibility, communication, teamwork, and response to feedback in a work-like setting. Recruiters, business owners, and other viewers can observe evidence that a CV or conventional interview may not reveal. The concept tests whether this kind of evidence helps employers notice and assess early-career talent.

For the initial MVP, the planned definite winner benefits are interview and presentation grooming and a CV review/building session. A short job-shadowing or internship opportunity, a paid role, and cash bounties are possible later outcomes, subject to company agreement and resources. None is guaranteed by the current concept. The project is not a job-placement guarantee and cannot solve unemployment on its own.

## 2. Problem and context

### The problem being explored

Some graduates have relevant knowledge, project experience, and the ability to learn, but have limited formal work history. Employers may have little comparable evidence of how those candidates approach an unfamiliar task, communicate trade-offs, or respond to feedback. A CV and a conventional interview can leave those capabilities difficult to assess.

Think You Can Build proposes making a candidate's problem-solving process visible through a structured, work-related challenge. It aims to add evidence to an employer's decision, not replace interviews, qualifications, references, or other hiring checks.

### South African context

Statistics South Africa's Q2 2026 Quarterly Labour Force Survey reported an official unemployment rate of 33.6% and a graduate unemployment rate of 12.4%. In the same quarter, the rate for people with other tertiary qualifications was 24.0%. Stats SA reports these education categories separately, so “graduates” should not be used to imply that every diploma or certificate holder is represented by the 12.4% figure. These figures establish labour-market context; they do not show that lack of practical evidence causes graduate unemployment or that this project will reduce it.

The founder also reports a personal observation: several people from their college cohort and the cohort one year below were not working in the field they studied in 2026, despite graduating around 2019/2020. The cohort sizes and counts need clarification before quoting them numerically. This is a motivating observation, not a representative survey.

### Problem statement

How might employers see credible evidence of what graduates can do before those graduates have had a chance to build formal work experience, while keeping the assessment fair, useful, and safe for participants?

## 3. Stakeholders and influence

| Stakeholder | What they need or contribute | Influence over the system |
|---|---|---|
| Graduates / participants | A fair chance to demonstrate skills, clear expectations, useful feedback, and control over what personal information is made public | Decide whether to participate; their work and experience determine whether the format is credible |
| Employers / challenge sponsors | Relevant problem-solving evidence and a clear way to submit or validate a case | Can supply real cases, validate solutions, offer interviews or opportunities, and fund agreed bounties |
| Facilitator / project lead | Challenge brief, session plan, safeguarding and moderation procedures, and a scoring rubric | Shapes the prompts, what is visible, and how work is assessed |
| Reviewers / mentors | Consistent scoring guidance and enough evidence to review the strongest submissions | Influence shortlisting, winner selection, and participant development |
| Viewers / recruiters / entrepreneurs | A clear view of candidate work and a way to discover talent | May create exposure or opportunities; public comments can also create pressure or popularity bias |
| Founder / production team | Platform, challenge sourcing, operations, moderation, consent process, and follow-up | Controls the format and is accountable for making the pilot feasible and responsible |
| Graduate-focused recruitment partners (future) | Defined partnership terms and a credible candidate evidence format | Could help source participants or employers if a partnership is agreed |

Employers and hiring managers hold much of the decision-making power over access to jobs. The project can create another source of evidence, but it cannot require employers to change their hiring process or offer an opportunity.

## 4. Current system, root causes, and assumptions

### How the current loop can work

1. Entry-level roles request experience or strong evidence of readiness.
2. Graduates without formal work history have fewer examples to present.
3. CV screening and short interviews give employers limited time to observe practical work.
4. Employers may rely more on familiar signals such as past job titles, referrals, or institution names.
5. Graduates who do not get an initial opportunity have fewer chances to build the experience employers request.

This is the project's working system map, based on the challenge framing and founder observations. It needs validation with graduates and employers. It should not be described as a proven explanation for national unemployment.

### Root causes the project will investigate

- Limited access to a first role can make it harder to accumulate professional evidence.
- Employers may lack time or tools to assess candidates consistently.
- CVs and interviews may not reveal a candidate's practical reasoning or learning process.
- Networks and exposure can affect who hears about opportunities and whose work gets noticed.
- Graduates may lack structured practice presenting and defending their work.

### Key assumptions

- Employers and recruiters will watch or review challenge evidence when it is relevant and concise.
- Observing work in a realistic task provides useful additional evidence beyond a CV and interview.
- A facilitator can help reveal a candidate's reasoning without solving the problem for them.
- Participants will consent to a public stream because exposure is a central part of the concept.
- A mock case can be realistic enough to test relevant skills without exposing confidential company information.
- A repeatable rubric can help reviewers assess candidates consistently.
- A defined target field can be chosen after surveying graduates and potential employers.

These are hypotheses, not findings. The first pilot should test them before the project invests in a full platform or promises business outcomes.

## 5. Proposed intervention: how a challenge works

### Participant experience

1. A graduate registers and sees the case brief, expected skills, session format, public-stream conditions, scoring rubric, and any participation requirements before agreeing to take part.
2. Three to five graduates join one facilitated call and attempt the same challenge individually.
3. Each participant explains their understanding, asks questions, demonstrates attempts, and presents a proposed solution. Participants can learn from new information and discussion as the session proceeds, as they might in a workplace.
4. The facilitator uses consistent prompts and makes sure each participant has an opportunity to contribute. The facilitator does not provide unequal hints or quietly complete work for a participant.
5. The facilitator scores each submission using the published rubric. A second reviewer independently reviews the strongest submissions. Scores and written evidence are discussed to determine a shortlist or winner.
6. Shortlisted graduates review the solutions with the facilitator. This self-review is developmental; it does not replace independent scoring.
7. Viewers can comment, subject to moderation. Viewer comments are not part of the winner score in the MVP, to avoid popularity and harassment effects.
8. The winner receives interview and presentation grooming plus a CV review/building session. With permission and a suitable company relationship, a later business pitch may be arranged.

### Challenge sourcing

**Initial phase:** Use fictional mock companies and hypothetical or adapted business cases. Do not imply that a real company supplied, approved, or validated a case unless it has done so and given permission. Avoid confidential, personal, or commercially sensitive information.

**Later phase:** Invite businesses to submit a challenge. Agree the scope, permitted information, review role, intellectual property terms, bounty (if any), and what outcomes the business is willing to consider before the case is published. The proposal to approach at least 20 companies in the launch month is an outreach target. It is not evidence of company interest or a commitment to provide challenges.

### Draft assessment rubric

Use a 1–5 scale for each criterion, with written evidence for the score. Equal weighting is a simple starting point for a pilot; reviewers should revisit it after testing. The rubric assesses demonstrated work in this challenge, not a person's overall worth or future potential.

| Criterion | What reviewers look for |
|---|---|
| Problem understanding | Clarifies the user or business need, constraints, and what success would mean |
| Reasoning | Explains choices, tests assumptions, and uses evidence or feedback to improve the approach |
| Feasibility | Proposes a solution that could reasonably be implemented, with relevant trade-offs acknowledged |
| Communication | Explains the approach and result clearly, and responds constructively to questions |
| Teamwork / collaboration | Listens, shares useful ideas, builds on others' contributions, and disagrees respectfully |
| Learning from feedback | Adapts when new information appears and can explain what changed |

The challenge is an individual attempt within a shared group session. Reviewers should record individual evidence so that airtime, confidence, or another participant's performance does not stand in for the candidate's contribution. Publish challenge-specific accommodations and scoring guidance before the session.

## 6. MVP scope and feasibility

### What the first MVP should prove

The MVP is a small, manually operated pilot, not a complete recruitment platform. It should test whether the format can be run safely and whether graduates, reviewers, and prospective employers find the evidence useful.

**In scope:**

- One computer-based mock-company challenge in a field selected after discovery research.
- Three to five graduate participants making individual attempts in a shared call.
- One facilitator and at least one additional reviewer.
- A published brief, consent process, rubric, moderation rules, and post-challenge feedback.
- A live stream or recording workflow using existing video tools, after the participant process and risk review are ready.
- A simple landing page or dashboard that explains the project and links to the challenge and replay.
- Interview/presentation grooming and CV review/building as the planned winner benefits, subject to the founder being able to provide them.

**Out of scope for the first MVP:**

- Automated candidate ranking or AI scoring.
- A custom video-hosting service.
- Guaranteed job offers, internships, cash prizes, or company interviews.
- A full company bounty marketplace or a claim of 20 confirmed business partners.
- A bespoke remote OBS control portal unless the pilot proves it is needed.

### Technical approach

The proposed web application uses the MERN stack: MongoDB for data storage, Express and Node.js for the server/backend, and React for the user-facing website. Supporting tools under consideration include Teams or Google Meet for the facilitated call, OBS or StreamYard for production, and YouTube for hosting public recordings. These choices are provisional until tested against the pilot workflow and privacy requirements.

For the MVP, a hosted call and video service are likely more feasible than building custom video infrastructure. YouTube may reduce hosting complexity, but public hosting, comments, discoverability, retention, and monetization need deliberate settings and participant disclosure. YouTube revenue is uncertain and should not be included as an expected MVP funding source.

AI tools such as GPT, Claude, and Lovable may support design and development. They are development aids, not core product components. Human facilitators and reviewers remain responsible for challenge delivery and evaluation. Do not put participant personal data or confidential business material into AI tools without an approved data-handling basis.

### People and operations

The founder is currently the only team member. A future operating team may include:

- An experienced facilitator/mentor for each stream.
- A person responsible for employer outreach, challenge sourcing, and bounty negotiation.
- An HR or career-development partner for candidate grooming and employer follow-up.
- Production and moderation support as stream volume grows.

For a single initial pilot, roles may be combined if the founder has the skills and time, but independent review and moderation should not be dropped. No partners or contracted roles are secured yet.

## 7. Evidence, research, and validation plan

### Evidence available now

- **Official context:** Stats SA Q2 2026 data reports 12.4% unemployment among graduates and 33.6% overall. People with other tertiary qualifications are reported separately at 24.0%.
- **Founder observation:** The founder has seen people from their college cohorts struggle to work in their field after graduation. The numbers are not yet clear and the observation is not representative.
- **No primary project research yet:** No graduate survey, employer interviews, prototype tests, pilot results, or confirmed company partnerships have been completed.

### Discovery before launch

Run short, voluntary discovery interviews or a survey with recent graduates and employers in potential computer-based fields. Ask about recent hiring screens, what evidence employers trust, what candidates find hard to demonstrate, and whether a recorded challenge would be useful. Do not treat survey responses as proof of hiring impact.

Choose the first field based on a combination of:

- Graduate interest and reported difficulty accessing relevant work.
- Employer demand and willingness to review work samples.
- Whether a fair, realistic case can be completed with a computer and common tools.
- Whether the challenge can be made accessible without requiring expensive software, equipment, or unpaid preparation.

The claim that one field has “the most unemployed graduates” needs a reliable, field-specific data source before it is used to select the pilot. The current Stats SA figures by education level do not identify a field with the highest graduate unemployment.

### Pilot test

After the hackathon prototype, run a small pilot only after the brief, consent, rubric, moderation, and data-handling process are ready. Collect feedback from participants, reviewers, and invited employer viewers. Compare rubric scores across reviewers and record where reviewers disagree. Ask employer viewers whether they learned something decision-relevant that they would not have learned from the candidate's CV alone.

## 8. Success measures and expected impact

### Near-term measures

- Number of graduates who express interest, register, consent, attend, and complete the challenge.
- Participant understanding of the brief, rubric, and recording/publication terms.
- Completion and withdrawal rates, with reasons where participants choose to share them.
- Reviewer agreement on rubric scores and examples of evidence cited for each score.
- Whether each participant gets a fair opportunity to speak and demonstrate work.
- Feedback from graduates and reviewers on usefulness, pressure, accessibility, and fairness.
- Number of employer conversations, case submissions, and follow-up reviews; report outreach separately from confirmed commitments.
- Whether an employer viewer requests a follow-up conversation or interview, without implying that a request is a job outcome.

### Longer-term outcomes to track

- Participants who receive a relevant interview, shadowing opportunity, internship, or job, with participant consent and clear definitions.
- Whether participating employers use challenge evidence in a documented hiring decision.
- Participant confidence and ability to explain work in later interviews.
- Outcomes by relevant demographic groups, where collection is lawful, voluntary, secure, and large enough to interpret responsibly.

Early success is evidence that candidates and employers find the format useful, and that assessment can be run consistently and safely. Views, likes, and comments are engagement measures, not proof of employability impact.

## 9. Risks, trade-offs, and safeguards

| Risk / trade-off | Why it matters | Proposed response |
|---|---|---|
| Public exposure may deter or disadvantage some graduates | Camera comfort, disability, safety, internet access, or personal circumstances can affect participation independently of ability | Explain the public format before registration; explore a private or recorded alternative before launch. If public streaming remains mandatory, state the exclusion clearly and avoid claiming the pilot represents all graduates |
| Consent may not fully address power or future use | Participants may feel they must agree for access to an opportunity; public video can be copied or viewed later | Use plain-language consent and separate permissions for recording, publication, clips, and future reuse. Explain withdrawal limits after public publication. Get appropriate review of consent and data-handling materials before streaming |
| Bias in live presentation and group dynamics | Confidence, accent, airtime, neurodiversity, and familiarity with streaming may affect perceptions | Publish a rubric; train facilitators; use consistent prompts; record individual evidence; provide equal turns and reasonable accommodations; use independent review |
| Viewer comments can become harmful or distort decisions | Comments can expose participants to harassment or popularity scoring | Moderate or disable comments; do not include viewer popularity in official scores; provide a reporting route and removal process |
| The case is not genuinely representative of workplace work | A staged challenge may reward showmanship or a narrow task skill | Describe it as one sample of work, not a complete job-readiness test; use employer input to refine cases; test whether rubric scores inform actual hiring decisions |
| Business cases expose confidential information or imply endorsement | Adapted cases may still reveal sensitive details or create a false company association | Start with fictional mock companies; get written permission for any named or real company case; remove sensitive data and explain provenance |
| Intellectual property expectations are unclear | Participants and businesses may disagree about ownership or use of solutions | Publish plain-language terms before the challenge covering participant work, business use, reuse, attribution, and any bounty. Obtain qualified advice before using real business challenges |
| Bounty, internship, or job promises are not fulfilled | Overstated rewards can damage trust and create pressure | Advertise only confirmed rewards; negotiate any bounty before publishing the challenge; state that employment outcomes depend on the employer and are not guaranteed |
| Platform and operating workload grows too soon | Custom streaming, moderation, storage, and recruitment operations can exceed a solo founder's capacity | Use a small, manually run pilot with existing tools; limit frequency; add people and automation only after workflow evidence supports it |
| Monetization assumptions fail | YouTube revenue depends on channel eligibility, audience, and platform rules | Treat any future revenue as uncertain; do not rely on it to fund the initial pilot |
| Privacy and data security are inadequate | Registration data, recordings, and evaluation notes can be sensitive | Collect only necessary data, restrict access, define retention/deletion rules, and review the process against applicable South African privacy requirements before collecting or publishing personal information |

## 10. Limitations and boundaries

- The concept is at the idea stage; no prototype or pilot has been tested yet.
- National unemployment statistics do not establish why individual graduates are unemployed.
- The project will initially reach graduates willing and able to appear on a public stream; this can exclude people and limit how representative the pilot is.
- A challenge demonstrates performance on one task under specific conditions. It cannot establish a candidate's full capability, reliability, or suitability for every role.
- Employers may watch without hiring, and hiring depends on factors outside the project's control.
- The project has no confirmed company partners, challenge submissions, bounty funding, recruitment partners, or contracted facilitators.
- The founder is currently operating alone, which limits pilot scale and requires careful scope control.
- Public visibility may produce exposure but also creates privacy, safety, moderation, and reputational risks.

## 11. Immediate next steps

1. Build a clickable or static concept prototype for the hackathon, clearly marked as a prototype.
2. Create one fictional, computer-based challenge brief and test that three to five individual attempts can fit a manageable session.
3. Draft the 1–5 rubric, facilitator guide, reviewer sheet, and participant feedback form.
4. Survey or interview graduates and employers to select the first field and test interest in reviewing challenge evidence.
5. Define the public recording, consent, moderation, data retention, and intellectual property approach before inviting participants.
6. Approach companies with a clear later-stage offer; track contacts, conversations, and confirmed commitments separately. Treat 20 company approaches in the first month as a target.
7. Run one small pilot, review what worked and what did not, then decide whether to build the full React/MERN dashboard.
